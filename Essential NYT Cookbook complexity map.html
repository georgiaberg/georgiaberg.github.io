Effective complexity (vertical, default)

Gell-Mann and Lloyd define it as the length of the shortest description of an object's regularities, leaving out the random part. Here the regularities are what a cook must specify to get this dish rather than another one. The score counts them:

ingredients + 2 × techniques + 4 × extra components + 3 × cross-references

Techniques come from a list of 54 (braise, temper, laminate, emulsify, knife work and so on) matched against the method. Components are the "For the sauce" style sub-lists, plus any sub-recipe that follows the main one. Cross-references are ingredients that point to another recipe in the book, the cookbook version of a subroutine call.

Algorithmic complexity (horizontal, default)

How prep time grows with n, the number of people you feed. Each recipe gets every growth term its method shows, and it is placed by the fastest-growing one.

  • O(1): chunks in one pot or pan. Twice the stew in a bigger pot cooks in the same time, because the pieces stay the same size.
  • O(log n): a starter, biga or poolish, which doubles with each feeding. Only 4 recipes have one, and all 4 also shape loaves by hand, so none of them sits in this band. It shows up as a secondary term.
  • O(n^1/3): finished by reducing a liquid, setting a jam, or cooking to syrup. Evaporation runs through the surface, and in a pot scaled up in proportion, volume over surface grows like n^1/3. In the same pan it would be O(n).
  • O(n^2/3): one mass heated through, such as a whole bird, a roast checked by thermometer, a terrine, or a cake in a loaf, tube or springform pan. Heat diffusion time goes as thickness squared, and thickness as n^1/3.
  • O(n/k): limited by capacity. It cooks in batches or a single layer, or has a little per-piece work.
  • O(n) plate: portioned and finished one serving at a time, such as ramekins, garnishing each plate, or a cocktail built glass by glass.
  • O(n) hand: pieces made by hand, such as shaping, filling, piping, crimping or cutting out.

Nothing in the book is superlinear in servings. That only happens at the level of the meal, where cooks coordinate with each other.

Time and the other metrics

Total time adds up every duration written into the method, using the midpoint of ranges, 10 hours for "overnight", and skipping storage times like "keeps for a week". It then adds an estimate of active prep: 1.5 minutes per ingredient and 1 minute per step. Hands-off time is the part spent resting, chilling, rising, marinating or soaking. Steps that overlap get counted twice, so long braises and preserves run high.

The Kolmogorov proxy is the gzip-compressed size of the ingredient list plus the method. That is the total description length, prose and all, so it's a rough stand-in for the algorithmic information content Gell-Mann and Lloyd start from.

What this bends

  • Big O is about n going to infinity. Potlucks have n of about 20, so the constants that Big O drops are exactly what decides your evening.
  • "O(1)" pretends pots never fill up. Really it is a step function.
  • O(n/k) and both O(n) bands are the same class to a theorist. Keeping them apart sorts them by their constants, which Big O exists to ignore.
  • Effective complexity depends on the reader: "make a roux" is one line for some cooks and a whole subroutine for others. The score treats the book's own wording as the reference machine.
  • Every number here is read from the text by pattern matching, so any single dot can be wrong. Trust the shape of the cloud more than any one point.
